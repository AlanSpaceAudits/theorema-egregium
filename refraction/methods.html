Working Around Refraction

How 21 of the 21 triangulation surveys (1739 to 1908) dealt with atmospheric refraction in their horizontal angles: not by a computed correction, but by how and when they observed. Each passage is quoted from the printed record with its page. Companion to Refraction in the Surveys.
Five methods. Steady-air hours · Night lamps and heliotropes · Towers, scaffolds and high stations · Repetition on different days and hours · Rejecting readings in unsteady air. A lateral bend of a sight line comes from the air along that line at that moment, so no table can predict it. The surveys removed it by choosing the air they observed through and by averaging over many states of it.

Steady-air hours

They observed in the hours when the air near the ground was calmest, and stopped when it was not. 18 of 21 surveys record it.

Delambre and Méchain, meridian arc Dunkirk to Barcelona (Base du système métrique) · 1792-1798

horizontal angles Possible lateral refraction: reverberatory lamps would be equally subject to it; the two remedies are to wait for favourable moments and to repeat observations at different hours and circumstances.
“S'il existe une réfraction latérale , comme j'ai été par fois tenté de le croire , les réverbères y seroient également sujets , et l'on n'a pour se garantir de cette illusion que deux moyens qui ne sont pas infaillibles , l'un est d'attendre les instans favorables ; l'autre , de répéter les observations à des heures et dans des circonstances différentes.”
In English: “If a lateral refraction exists, as I have at times been tempted to believe, reverberatory lamps would be equally subject to it, and there are only two means, not infallible, to guard against this illusion: one is to wait for favourable moments; the other, to repeat the observations at different hours and in different circumstances.”
Méchain & Delambre (1806), T1 Discours préliminaire p. 106
Méchain, P., & Delambre, J.-B. J. (1806). Base du système métrique décimal, ou mesure de l'arc du méridien compris entre les parallèles de Dunkerque et Barcelone (Vol. 1). Paris: Baudouin.
horizontal angles Rieupeyroux area (La Bastide/Aubassin): if increasing evening refraction raised each signal by one minute, the reduced angle would fall by 2.8 seconds.
“Le soir approchoit. Supposons que la réfraction terrestre augmentant , ait élevé les signaux chacun d'une minute , l'angle réduit à l'horizon diminuera de 2"8”
In English: “Evening was approaching. Suppose that the increasing terrestrial refraction raised the signals one minute each; the angle reduced to the horizon will decrease by 2"8”
Méchain & Delambre (1806), T1 p. 272 · text layer

vertical angles Montjouy sea-horizon series, all near sunset, disagree with the levelling; Delambre states that refraction must have raised the sea more than in the mean state; one isolated sea-horizon observation can be wrong by 5 to 6 toises; the levelling is kept.
“toutes ces observations ayant été faites vers le coucher du soleil , la réfraction devoit élever la mer plus que dans l'état moyen et faire paroître la hauteur de la tour trop petite . In English: “all these observations having been made towards sunset, refraction must have raised the sea more than in the mean state and made the height of the tower appear too small. They prove that an isolated observation of the sea horizon can give an error of 5 to 6 toises.”

vertical angles Evening effect: the Boiscommun steeple rose into view at sunset; terrestrial refraction raises the same object more or less at different times and hides or reveals it.
“Cet effet des réfractions terrestres qui , à différens temps ou à différentes heures du même jour , et , suivant les variations atmosphériques , élève plus ou moins le même objet , le cache ou le rend visible , est singulièrement curieux”
In English: “This effect of terrestrial refractions which, at different times or different hours of the same day, and according to atmospheric variations, raises the same object more or less, hides it or makes it visible, is singularly curious”

vertical angles One series of ten for each zenith distance, unless something made the observation doubtful. In fine weather Delambre preferred to repeat the angles of position; in bad weather, he says, the variations of terrestrial refraction would have made the zenith distances more uncertain. Distances above 91 or below 89 degrees should be observed at the same time as the angle, which was hardly possible, so they were taken just before or after, or at several hours of the day.
“Quand le temps étoit beau , j'aimois mieux répéter l'observation des angles de position , et , s'il étoit mauvais , les variations de la réfraction terrestre auroient rendu les distances au zénith plus incertaines.”
In English: “When the weather was fine, I preferred to repeat the observation of the angles of position, and, if it was bad, the variations of terrestrial refraction would have made the zenith distances more uncertain.”

In English: “in order to choose for the reduction of the angles to the horizon the distance whose hour was nearest, and where one had reason to believe terrestrial refraction least different.”

Parallel of Paris, Brest to Strasbourg (Henry east part, Bonne west part), Nouvelle carte de France · 1818-1823

horizontal angles Night observations with lamps on this line, shortly after sunset, often gave much larger discordances than day observations.
“C'est sur une grande partie de cette ligne, confiée, dès l'origine des travaux, à MM. les colonels Bonne et Henry, que des réverbères ont été employés pour signaux; mais les observations nocturnes, à peu d'intervalle du coucher du soleil, ont souvent présenté des discordances beaucoup plus considérables que celles faites de jour.”
In English: “It is on a large part of this line, entrusted from the start of the work to Colonels Bonne and Henry, that lamps were used as signals; but the night observations, shortly after sunset, often showed much larger discordances than those made by day.”
Puissant (1832), 35
Puissant, L. (1832). Nouvelle description géométrique de la France (Première partie). Paris: Ch. Picquet.
vertical angles Instruction: zenith distances in at least three series at different hours, above all near midday, simultaneous where possible, with barometer readings.
“Les distances zénithales relatives à ces chaînes seront elles-mêmes le résultat de trois séries au moins, prises à des heures différentes, et surtout vers le milieu du jour, afin qu'elles puissent donner les différences de niveau avec toute l'exactitude désirable, et, autant qu'il sera possible, par le concours d'observations simultanées : celles-ci seront accompagnées d'observations barométriques.”
In English: “The zenith distances for these chains will themselves be the result of at least three series, taken at different hours, and above all towards the middle of the day, so that they can give the level differences with all desirable accuracy, and, as far as possible, by the combination of simultaneous observations: these will be accompanied by barometric observations.”
Puissant (1832), 28
Puissant, L. (1832). Nouvelle description géométrique de la France (Première partie). Paris: Ch. Picquet.

“on prit la résolution, ainsi qu'il a été déjà signalé page 32, de multiplier les observations d'angles entre les objets terrestres trente fois au moins par chaque série, et de répéter ces séries à des heures et à des jours différens.”

Trigonometrical Survey of England and Wales (Ordnance), Roy 1784-1788 and Williams, Mudge and Dalby 1791-1799 · 1784-1799

horizontal angles Roy: in hot weather the air was too tremulous for accurate pointing except for short periods in the mornings and evenings; a whole day could pass for one good observation.
“At particular times, especially in hot weather, there was such a tremulous motion or boiling in the air, that it was only during a very short space, chiefly in the mornings and evenings, that the objects were sufficiently distinct to be observed with accuracy. So difficult it is to do any thing perfectly good in this way, that a whole day has frequently been spent, after watching with anxious care, in obtaining a single one that was perfectly satisfactory!”
Roy (1790), p. 170

horizontal angles Williams, Mudge and Dalby 1795: bearings of intersected objects were taken in the morning or evening, free of vapour and of the midday quivering.
“That the bearings of those might be taken with precision, the observations were made either in the morning or evening, when the air was free from vapour, and without that quivering motion, which, in summer, it generally has in the middle of the day.”

Ordnance Trigonometrical Survey of Great Britain and Ireland, Principal Triangulation · 1783-1853 (published 1858)

vertical angles Daily variation: refraction greater by night; early-morning zenith distances are smaller; no vertical observations should be taken early in the morning; Hart Fell showed double refraction from 1 to 3 p.m. in September.
“When observations are recorded as having been taken at some early hour in the morning, as 5 A.M., and from that time until 8 A.M., they almost invariably show a smaller zenith distance than during any other part of the day; sometimes the difference is very considerable, and shows that no vertical observations should be taken early in the morning.”

“The great amount of refraction in the morning, its diminution towards the middle of the day, and its increase again towards evening, is obviously caused by the greater amount of aqueous vapour in the lower portion of the atmosphere in the morning and evening, as compared with the amount in the middle of the day.”
