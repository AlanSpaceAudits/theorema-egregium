Évaux to Carcassonne

Does the sky match the ground in Delambre and Méchain's meridian arc (1792 to 1798)? A check of one claim against the printed record.
The claim. "From Évaux to Carcassonne the physically measured distance is 350 km. Astronomical latitude says it is 330 km." That is a mismatch of 20 km, about 6%.
The result. The printed ground distance is 168,846.7 toises = 329.088 km, not 350 km. The sky and the ground agree to within 117 to 250 m (3.8″ to 8.1″ of latitude), depending on which degree length is used. A 20 km mismatch would need an error of 648″ (10.8′) in the latitudes. Each latitude here is the mean of hundreds of star observations, and along the whole arc the latitudes never disagree with the ground by more than a few seconds. Delambre himself discussed the few-second disagreement and Legendre put it down to local attraction on the plumb line.

1. What the book prints

Volume 3 sets out the four sections of the arc. For each one it gives the ground arc in toises (from the triangles), the difference of the astronomical latitudes, and the length of one degree that follows from the two.

"L'arc entre Évaux et Carcassonne est de 168846.7, et l'arc céleste de 2° 57′ 48″24. Le degré sera donc de 56977.80 à la latitude moyenne de 44° 41′ 48″37."

In English: "The arc between Évaux and Carcassonne is 168,846.7, and the celestial arc is 2° 57′ 48.24″. The degree is therefore 56,977.80 at the mean latitude 44° 41′ 48.37″."

The ground distance is therefore 329.088 km. No figure on these pages gives 350 km. The stations are close to one meridian (Évaux and Carcassonne differ by about 0.14° of longitude), so the straight line between them is only about 0.2 km longer than the meridian arc.

One small print slip: 168,846.7 divided by 2° 57′ 48.24″ gives 56,977.36, not the printed 56,977.80. The difference is 0.0008% and does not change anything below.

2. Where the two latitudes come from

Each end latitude is a long series of star observations with the Borda repeating circle.

The daily results at Évaux scatter by a few seconds from night to night, and the two stars agree with each other to about half a second. That is the size of error these latitudes carry: seconds, not minutes.

3. The honest sky and ground test

The sky gives only an angle: Évaux is 2° 57′ 48.24″ north of Carcassonne. To turn that angle into a distance, a length for one degree is needed. That length must come from somewhere other than this section's own ground arc. If it comes from the same ground arc, the test is circular: dividing the ground arc by the angle and then multiplying it back always returns the ground arc. (A chatbot answer that found "2.6 metres" did exactly that; the 2.6 m is only rounding.)

In every case the ground arc is a little shorter than the sky predicts, by about 120 to 250 m over 329 km, or 4 to 8 seconds of latitude. That is the real mismatch in this section, and it is 80 to 170 times smaller than 20 km.

4. What Delambre and Legendre said about it

Delambre noticed that the degree does not shrink smoothly from north to south. His table's last column gives the drop per degree: 5.5, then 32.4, then 12.9 toises.

"La seconde est que cette diminution assez irrégulière n'est pas celle que donneroit la courbure elliptique du méridien."

In English: "The second [remark] is that this rather irregular decrease is not the one the elliptical curvature of the meridian would give." On the same page he shows that raising the latitude of Montjouy by 3.24″, to agree with Méchain's observations in Barcelona, makes the southern section fit much better.

"... en supposant l'aplatissement 1/320, la correction de la latitude de Paris seroit 0″00, et celle de Montjouy + 3″62 ... Pour Carcassonne, la correction seroit − 0″88; pour Dunkerque + 3″06, et pour Évaux − 5″83. Je ne vois aucune objection à faire à ces résultats quand on les attribuera, comme a fait M. Legendre, à des attractions locales qui agissent irrégulièrement sur le fil à plomb."

In English: "Supposing the flattening 1/320, the correction to the latitude of Paris would be 0.00″, and that of Montjouy +3.62″. For Carcassonne the correction would be −0.88″; for Dunkerque +3.06″, and for Évaux −5.83″. I see no objection to these results when they are put down, as M. Legendre did, to local attractions that act irregularly on the plumb line." The same page gives the flattening that best fits the five French latitudes on their own: 1/150 (Laplace) and 1/148 (Legendre).

So the sky and the ground disagree in this arc by a few seconds of latitude at single stations. Évaux, in the Massif Central, carries the largest correction, −5.83″, about 180 m. The book prints these numbers openly and discusses them.

5. A second kind of check: azimuths

Delambre also compared the azimuth (the direction from north) observed at a station with the Sun or stars against the azimuth carried to that station through the triangles.

At Carcassonne the computed azimuth of Nore is 201° 19′ 36.9″ and the observed one is 201° 18′ 58.0″, an excess of 38.9″. The page then lists the differences between pairs of stations: for example +39.4″ from Paris to Bourges and +45.7″ from Paris to Carcassonne.

"Les deux azimuts auxquels j'aurois le plus de confiance, par le nombre et la qualité des observations, sont précisément ceux qui diffèrent le plus. Il est difficile de méconnoître ici l'effet de l'irrégularité des parallèles."

In English: "The two azimuths I would trust most, by the number and quality of the observations, are exactly the ones that differ most. It is hard not to see here the effect of the irregularity of the parallels." These are differences of direction of tens of seconds. They do not enter the north to south distance between Évaux and Carcassonne, which comes from the latitudes in sections 2 and 3.

Between the parallels: 446,084.7 − 277,237.9 = 168,846.8 toises, the same as the arc on p. 89. The straight line from the rectangular figures: √(5,468.2² + 168,821.1²) = 168,909.6 toises = 329.2 km. Both ways the ground distance is 329 km.

Worked out here from the printed rows. The straight line is longer than the meridian arc by only 123 m because the two stations are 5,468 toises (10.7 km) apart east to west.

A trap that gives about 350 km. When we first laid the triangles out on a plane from their printed sides, our own layout returned 350.3 km for Évaux to Carcassonne. The check of every printed side then showed misfits of up to 272%: some triangles had been placed mirror-wise across a shared side. One mirrored triangle anywhere in the chain moves every station after it, so a figure near 350 km can come from a layout error, not from the survey. The printed table above needs no layout at all.

Timing. The latitudes come from star altitudes near the meridian, not from clock times. A clock error only changes the small reduction of each altitude to the meridian (+24.66″ in the Carcassonne example of 23 March 1797), and stars observed on both sides of the meridian cancel that error to first order. Delambre and Méchain also took the Pole Star at both its upper and lower transits, which removes an error in the star's catalogue place. A clock error of several seconds therefore moves a latitude by well under a second. It cannot produce the 648″ that a 20 km mismatch needs. Clock times matter for longitude, and the north to south distance between Évaux and Carcassonne does not use longitude.

7. Where 350 km could come from

No printed figure in the three volumes gives 350 km for Évaux to Carcassonne. Some numbers that might be mixed up with it:

  • The road distance between the two towns, which is longer than any survey line.
  • The sum of the triangle sides along the chain, which zigzags and is much longer than the meridian arc.
  • A different section: Panthéon to Évaux is 152,293.1 toises = 296.8 km; Carcassonne to Montjouy is 205.6 km; Évaux to Montjouy is 534.7 km.

The claim needs its own source and working. Without them, the printed record shows a ground arc of 329.1 km and a sky that agrees with it to a few hundred metres.
